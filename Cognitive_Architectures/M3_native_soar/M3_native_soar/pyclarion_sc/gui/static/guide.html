Self-control in Clarion, explained from the start

This site has two tools. The Architecture page shows the inside of the model, one decision at a time. The Robot page shows the same model driving a cleaning robot. Read this page first; it takes about fifteen minutes.

What Clarion is

Clarion is a cognitive architecture: a theory of how the mind works, written precisely enough to run as a computer program. It was developed by Ron Sun. The research in this project uses it to explain self-control in people, and then to give a robot the same kind of self-control.

The central idea is that the mind works at two levels at the same time:

Top level: explicit

Knowledge you can put into words, like rules: "If granola is available, pick granola." It is slower and takes effort.

Bottom level: implicit

Knowledge you act on without words, like habits and gut preferences: "Chocolate looks good." It is fast and automatic. In the model it is a small neural network.

Most of the time the two levels agree. Self-control is about what happens when they don't.

The four parts

Clarion has four subsystems. Each one has a top level and a bottom level. The self-control work uses three of them directly; the fourth (memory) appears in one of the simulations.

Motivational subsystem (MS)
Holds the drives, the basic needs behind behaviour. A drive gets stronger when the situation is relevant to it (stimulus level) and when the person or robot has a stronger need for it (deficit). Robot example: the energy drive grows as the battery runs down.
Metacognitive subsystem (MCS)
The "manager". It turns drives into a goal, and it decides how much effort to put into explicit thinking. That amount is the explicitness level, a number between 0 (all habit) and 1 (all plan).
Action-centered subsystem (ACS)
Chooses the action. The top level has rules (the plan). The bottom level has a neural network that scores each action (the habit). Explicitness decides which of the two gets to act.
Non-action-centered subsystem (NACS)
Holds knowledge and memory. In one of the human simulations (Schmeichel, 2007) it is a working memory that only works well when the top level is in control.

Self-control in Clarion

In this framework, self-control is a decision about how explicit to be. When the plan and the habit want different things, the one with control wins. The manager (MCS) sets that balance by weighing benefit against cost:

  • Benefit: how much the current goal would satisfy the drives right now. This is value(g).
  • Cost: explicit thinking takes effort. This is the constant c.

If the benefit is bigger than the cost, higher explicitness is more useful, so the plan usually wins. If it is smaller, lower explicitness is more useful, so the habit usually wins. The choice is a little random, as human choices are: the model picks an explicitness level by chance, with better levels being more likely.

This one idea explains several human findings:

  • Anticipation (Myrseth et al., 2009): a choice that is still ahead makes the healthy goal feel more important, so control is stronger. After the choice, control relaxes.
  • Motivational shift (Schmeichel, 2007): after a long period of control, the drive behind it has been satisfied, its deficit is lower, and control is weaker on the next task.
  • Capacity (Friese et al., 2008): when the mind is busy (for example, memorizing eight digits), there is simply less room for explicit thinking, and habits take over.

The equations

Numbers refer to Gomez and Sun (2024). You can see each one computed with live numbers on the Architecture page.

WhatEquationIn words
Drive strength (Eq. 5)ds = stimulus level × deficitHow strongly a need is active right now.
Deficit decline (Eq. 6)deficit(t) = deficit(0) − k × time on taskSatisfying a need for a long time makes it weaker.
Goal strength (Eq. 7)gs = Σ relevance × dsA goal is strong when the drives behind it are strong.
Value of the goal (Eq. 3)value(g) = Σ ds × satisfactionHow much reaching the goal would satisfy current needs.
Utility of explicitness (Eq. 2)U = level × (α × value(g) − c)Benefit of explicit thinking minus its cost, for each level.
Choice (Eq. 4)p(level) ∝ eU/τBetter options are more likely, but not certain. The temperature τ sets how random.

Using the Architecture page

The Architecture page runs the decision cycle on its own, without a robot, so you can see every number. On the left you edit a scenario; on the right you see what the model does.

  • The diagram follows one cycle from left to right: the situation, the drives, the goal and explicitness, and the action. The blue band (plan) and orange band (habit) show how control is split; the dark line between them moves as explicitness changes.
  • The slider under the diagram steps through the cycles. Play runs them in order.
  • Over time shows drive strengths and explicitness for every cycle. A dot with a dark ring means the plan and the habit wanted different actions: a self-control conflict.
  • Inside cycle shows the equations with that cycle's numbers.

Three things to try

  1. Load "Myrseth et al. (2009): before choice", then "Myrseth et al. (2009): after choice". Compare the expected explicitness in the diagram (0.72 versus 0.47). Which numbers changed, and why?
  2. Load "Schmeichel (2007): attention control over time" and press Play. The achievement deficit falls each cycle (Eq. 6). Watch what happens to explicitness and to the share of orange dots.
  3. In any scenario, raise the cost c under Metacognition to 0.3. Predict what will happen before you look.

Using the Robot page

The Robot page puts the same cycle inside a cleaning robot. The robot must clean rooms A and B and return to its dock. Room C is very dirty but not its job: that is the temptation. The plan says "go to the next assigned room"; the habit network says "go where the dirt is".

  • The map is the main view. The trail is blue where the plan chose the path and orange where the habit did.
  • The right panel shows the robot's drives, its goal, the plan/habit split and its last decision.
  • The icons at the top open the rest: scenario and robot choice, an explanation of the scenario, charts, the robot's rules, and a comparison that asks whether self-control is useful for a robot.

There are three scenarios, one for each finding above, and three robots: the Clarion robot, a robot that always follows the plan, and a robot that always follows the habit. The two comparison robots show what self-control adds.

Three things to try

  1. In "Anticipation", pause just before the robot reaches room C's door and just after it. Compare the achievement drive and the plan/habit split.
  2. Watch the same seed with the habit-only robot, then the plan-only robot. How long does each take? What does each do at room C?
  3. In "Motivational shift", compare the two conditions. Where does the difference in the robot's behaviour appear, in room A or later?

Glossary

Drive
A basic need that motivates behaviour, such as energy or achievement.
Stimulus level
How relevant the current situation is to a drive (0 to 1).
Deficit
How strong the underlying need is for this person or robot (its "predisposition").
Goal
What the agent is trying to do right now, chosen from the drives.
value(g)
How much reaching the goal would satisfy the current drives.
Explicitness
The share of control given to the top level (the plan). 0 = all habit, 1 = all plan.
Top level
Explicit knowledge: rules that can be stated in words.
Bottom level
Implicit knowledge: a neural network that scores actions (Q-values) from experience.
Q-value
The bottom level's score for how good an action seems right now.
Boltzmann choice
Choosing by chance, where better options are more likely. Its temperature sets how random the choice is: low temperature, nearly always the best option.
Conflict
A cycle in which the plan and the habit propose different actions.
Safety reflex
In the robot, a fixed rule outside self-control: below 8% battery, go to the dock.
Seed
A number that fixes the random choices, so a run can be repeated exactly.
